Memory Model

FSRS Spaced Repetition for Chess

The mathematical formulation of FSRS-4.5/5 DSR power-law forgetting curves adapted for chess opening recall.

Source in app repo: ChessSRS Scheduling Architecture.md
App version: v1.4.0
Licence: GPL-3.0

1. Why FSRS for Chess Openings?

Traditional spaced repetition software relies on SM-2 (developed by SuperMemo in 1987) or basic exponential ladders. In flashcard vocabulary learning, SM-2 treats each word as an independent card with a single "ease factor".

In chess openings, SM-2 fails due to three fundamental flaws:

  1. Conflation of Difficulty and Stability: A move can be intrinsically difficult (counter-intuitive quiet move) while currently being very stable in your memory. SM-2's single scalar collapses these dimensions.
  2. Catastrophic Lapse Resets: If you forget move 8 of a sharp Sicilian line, SM-2 resets the interval to 1 day. In reality, your brain does not suffer total amnesia; with modern memory models, previous exposures provide residual stability.
  3. Arbitrary Target Retention: SM-2 cannot answer: "How frequently must I review this line so that I have a 95% probability of remembering it in tomorrow's tournament game?"

ChessSRS solves this by adopting the Free Spaced Repetition Scheduler (FSRS-4.5 / FSRS-5) continuous power-law model.

2. The DSR (Difficulty, Stability, Retrievability) Model

FSRS models human memory using three continuous variables:

  • Retrievability ($R \in [0, 1]$): The probability that you will successfully recall the correct repertoire move right now. Immediately after reviewing a move, $R = 1.0$. Over time $t$ (days), $R$ decays following a power-law curve.
  • Stability ($S \ge 0.1$): The durability of memory, defined as the time in days required for Retrievability to decay from 100% to 90% ($R(S) = 0.90$). Higher stability means slower memory decay.
  • Difficulty ($D \in [1, 10]$): The intrinsic complexity of the chess decision. Tactically natural moves have lower difficulty ($D \approx 2$); subtle positional moves or pawn breaks have higher difficulty ($D \approx 8$).

3. Mathematical Formulation

FSRS uses a continuous power-law forgetting curve derived from empirical cognitive memory datasets (>700M reviews):

Power-Law Retrievability Equation
R(t, S) = (1 + FACTOR · (t / S))^(-w)
Where:
• t = elapsed time in days since last active recall review
• S = memory stability in days
• FACTOR = (0.9^(-1/w) - 1), ensuring that R(S, S) ≡ 0.90
• w = power-law decay parameter (canonical default = 0.5)

Dynamic Interval Calculation for Target Retention

Given a player's desired retention $r_{ ext{target}}$ (configured in Settings, bounded between 0.70 and 0.97 per INV-RETENTION-BOUNDS), the exact due interval $I$ in days is solved analytically:

Optimal Review Interval Equation
I(r, S) = (S / FACTOR) · (r^(-1/w) - 1)
For target retention r = 0.90, the interval is exactly equal to stability: I(0.90, S) = S.
For target retention r = 0.95, reviews occur earlier: I(0.95, S) ≈ 0.47 · S.

4. Pure Binary Rating (Decision D015)

Canonical FSRS uses four buttons: Again (1), Hard (2), Good (3), Easy (4). In vocabulary flashcards, answer latency is often used to approximate ease.

The Chess Latency Fallacy

In chess, long think times represent disciplined tournament verification, double-checking tactical lines and avoiding blunders. Penalizing think time creates timer anxiety and trains reckless blitzing habits!

Therefore, ChessSRS strictly uses Pure Binary FSRS:

  • Again (Rating 1): Incorrect move played, hint requested, or pass. The position is registered as a lapse, stability undergoes a controlled haircut, and the card is re-queued in the intra-session buffer.
  • Good (Rating 3): Correct move played on first commit, regardless of whether it took 2 seconds or 60 seconds of calculation.

Think latency (latencyEmaMs) is tracked strictly for retrospective analytics and is completely firewalled from scheduling math.

5. Stability & Difficulty Updates

On Successful Recall (Good, G = 3)

Stability grows proportionally to difficulty, current stability, and retrieval effort:

S'(D, S, R) = S · (1 + e^(w8) · (11 - D) · S^(-w9) · (e^(w10 · (1 - R)) - 1))
Notice the term (1 - R): retrieving a move when Retrievability is low (high retrieval effort) yields a substantially larger stability increase than reviewing a move you just saw a few hours ago!

On Recall Lapse (Again, G = 1)

When a move is forgotten, stability does not collapse to zero; it resets to a controlled lower baseline:

S'(D, S, R) = w11 · D^(-w12) · ((S + 1)^w13 - 1) · e^(w14 · (1 - R))

Difficulty Mean Reversion

Difficulty adjusts smoothly with mean reversion to prevent extreme outliers:

D' = clip(w7 · D0(3) + (1 - w7) · (D - w6 · (G - 3)), 1.0, 10.0)
